ETHICAL DILEMA ANALYSIS

In 2005, Google acquired Android and wanted to build a mobile operating system that would attract a vast community of developers. To make the platform immediately familiar, Google engaged in negotiations to license Sun Microsystems' popular Java platform. When those licensing talks fell through, Google decided to forge ahead independently. They meticulously wrote their own "implementing code" from scratch. However, to ensure Java-trained developers could easily transition to Android, Google copied exactly 11,500 lines of declaring code across 37 Java APIs along with their unique structure, sequence, and organization (SSO). In 2010, Oracle bought Sun Microsystems and promptly launched a massive lawsuit against Google, seeking up to $9 billion in damages.

Analisys

The ethical analysis of this case can be viewed from two contrasting philosophical perspectives to understand what transpired. The first is utilitarian ethics—a results-oriented approach—which suggests that Google’s actions were ethical because they maximized public and professional benefit. By copying the 11,500 lines of code, Google enabled various developers to utilize the associated knowledge. What were the benefits? This prevented a monopoly on knowledge held by the original developers, accelerated mobile technology advancement, and created a massive, free open-source resource that benefited many consumers. The second perspective is deontological ethics—a duty-based approach. This view holds that Google’s actions were unethical; the company had a duty to respect the intellectual property and creative decisions of Sun/Oracle regarding the code's structure, sequence, and organization. When the license was denied, Google acted dishonestly, disregarding the fact that the code’s authority relied heavily on Sun—a decision that ultimately led to legal complications.

What I learned from this analysis is that intellectual property ethics rarely allow for a clear distinction between right and wrong. From a duty-based perspective, disregarding the agreements Google made to reuse code and structures protected by property rights is considered unethical. However, from a utilitarian perspective, reusing interface names to leverage developers' skills and expertise prevents monopolies in the information technology market, thereby enabling large-scale public innovation—an approach that the U.S. Supreme Court ultimately deemed legally permissible, or "fair use."

Ethical Dilema Alternative

Two ethical principles that greatly help me feel at peace—knowing the right thing was done—are honesty and fairness (or equitable treatment). One way to apply these principles is to first put myself in the other person's shoes to understand their needs and ensure fair treatment. In my work, I have often witnessed a lack of honest and fair treatment toward various employees, simply because supervisors struggle to communicate openly or empathize—that is, to put themselves in the other person's position. The Savior taught us this principle through one of the greatest acts of love ever known: laying down His life for all of Heavenly Father's children. He endured great pain with the sole intention of truly knowing each of us.

This helps me understand and apply this lesson today; it reminds me that we often must make sacrifices—such as taking extra time to resolve conflicts—but doing so brings peace of mind. It saves us from the guilt of inaction or the hollow satisfaction of merely managing a task without treating others fairly. That is why I believe this approach brings me peace with myself and others; knowing I am doing what is right, striving to understand the work involved—both my own and that of others—and truly valuing it.

Ethical Dilema

Reflecting on the ethical dilemma I cited as an example, I believe that plagiarism—specifically reusing third-party code without giving proper credit—violates moral and ethical values. In the field of computer science, one must exercise great care regarding code reuse; failing to attribute credit can lead to authorship disputes and is fundamentally dishonest. Based on the principles I have lived by; I know this would be detrimental; it would damage both one's reputation and self-image. Therefore, upholding principles of honesty and integrity is crucial when reusing code created by others.

Regarding the workplace, I believe that teamwork—whether as a developer, programmer, or systems technician—is essential for the team's success. When I need assistance with code or need to reuse existing code, I must be diligent about giving proper credit and being honest with my colleagues. Instead of claiming credit for work that isn't mine, I should prioritize a team-oriented mindset and open communication to prevent future issues. A common challenge in the workplace is the tendency to compare ourselves to others and undervalue our own potential. However, if we seek to expand our knowledge and improve in the tasks assigned by our employers, meeting high expectations becomes a matter of honest, diligent work. Possessing ethical and moral values is vital for facing these challenges; honesty—and avoiding the plagiarism of others' work—provides peace of mind that we are doing the right thing, while also fostering trust and a positive professional image among colleagues.
